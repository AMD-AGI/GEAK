A Short History of Keeping Time

For most of human history, the time of day was something you looked up at rather than down at. The sun rose, climbed, and fell, and the shadows it cast were the first clock anyone ever read. A stick pushed upright into the ground, a gnomon in the language of the Greeks, was enough to divide the day into a morning and an afternoon, and with a little patience and a few marks scratched into the soil it could divide the day into hours. The Egyptians built tall obelisks that served the same purpose on a civic scale, and by around 1500 BCE they had portable shadow clocks shaped like a letter L, which a priest or a traveller could turn to face the sun each morning and afternoon.

Shadow clocks had obvious weaknesses. They were useless at night and in cloudy weather, and the hours they marked were not equal. Because the length of daylight changes with the seasons, an hour defined as one twelfth of the daylight period was long in summer and short in winter. For most of antiquity nobody minded very much. Daily life was organised around daylight anyway, and an unequal hour that matched the working day was arguably more useful than an equal hour that did not.

Water offered a way to measure time without the sun. The simplest water clock, or clepsydra, was a vessel with a small hole near the bottom. As the water drained, the falling level could be read against marks on the inside wall. The Athenian law courts used such vessels to limit the length of speeches, and a speaker who ran long might be told that his water had run out. The phrase survives in several languages as a way of saying that someone's time is up. Engineers in Alexandria, and later in China and the Islamic world, built far more elaborate water clocks, with floats, gears, and figures that struck bells or opened doors at the hours. In the eleventh century, the Chinese official Su Song oversaw the construction of an astronomical clock tower more than ten metres high, driven by a water wheel that advanced in measured steps, an early form of what would later be called an escapement.

The escapement is the heart of every mechanical clock. A weight or a spring can supply energy, but on its own it would simply unwind as fast as friction allowed. The escapement releases that energy in small, regular portions, one tick at a time, and in doing so it turns a continuous force into a count. The earliest European mechanical clocks, which appeared in the late thirteenth century, used a verge and foliot escapement: a horizontal bar with weights at each end swung back and forth, and a toothed wheel advanced by one tooth with every swing. These clocks were large, iron, and not especially accurate. A good one might gain or lose a quarter of an hour in a day. They were usually installed in church towers and town halls, where they struck a bell rather than showing a dial, and the word clock itself comes from the old words for bell.

Inaccurate as they were, the tower clocks changed how people thought about the day. A bell that struck the same number of times at the same hour every day, regardless of the season, made the equal hour a public fact. Merchants, guilds, and city councils began to arrange their business by the clock rather than by the sun, and the unequal hours of antiquity slowly fell out of use.

The great leap in accuracy came from the pendulum. Galileo Galilei noticed, according to a story that may or may not be true, that a lamp swinging in the cathedral at Pisa seemed to take the same time for each swing whether the swing was wide or narrow. He later studied pendulums carefully and proposed using one to regulate a clock, but it was the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens who built the first working pendulum clock in 1656. The improvement was dramatic. Where earlier clocks drifted by many minutes a day, Huygens's clocks were accurate to within about a minute, and later refinements brought the error down to a few seconds. For the first time it became sensible to give clocks a minute hand, and soon afterwards a second hand.

Huygens also understood that a simple pendulum is not quite isochronous. A wide swing takes slightly longer than a narrow one, and the difference, though small, accumulates. He showed that a pendulum whose bob followed a curve called a cycloid would keep exactly equal time regardless of amplitude, and he fitted his clocks with curved metal cheeks to approximate that path. In practice, clockmakers found it simpler to keep the swing small and constant, and the anchor escapement, introduced around 1670, made that possible. With an anchor escapement and a long pendulum beating once a second, a well made clock could keep time to within a few seconds a week.

Accurate clocks on land did not solve the hardest timekeeping problem of the age, which was at sea. A navigator could find his latitude from the height of the sun at noon or of the pole star at night, but longitude was another matter. Because the earth turns fifteen degrees every hour, the difference between local time and the time at a reference meridian gives the longitude directly. Local time could be found from the sun. The time at the reference meridian had to be carried aboard the ship, and no pendulum could keep time on a rolling deck. Ships were lost, and with them cargoes and crews, because their navigators did not know where they were. In 1714 the British Parliament offered a prize of up to twenty thousand pounds for a practical method of finding longitude at sea.

The prize was eventually won, after decades of argument, by John Harrison, a self taught carpenter and clockmaker from Lincolnshire. Harrison's first marine timekeepers were large brass machines with balances linked by springs, designed so that the motion of the ship would affect them equally and cancel out. His fourth design, completed in 1759, looked instead like an oversized pocket watch, about thirteen centimetres across. On a voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762 it lost only about five seconds over more than two months, an error that corresponded to roughly a nautical mile of longitude. Harrison spent years persuading the Board of Longitude that his success was not luck, and he received the bulk of his reward only late in life, after the king himself intervened. Within a few decades, chronometers built on similar principles were standard equipment on ocean going ships.

Railways created a different kind of problem. In the early nineteenth century, every town kept its own local time, set by the sun. Bristol was about ten minutes behind London, and nobody in Bristol much cared. A railway timetable, however, could not cope with dozens of local times along a single line. British railway companies began to adopt London time for their operations in the 1840s, and the telegraph made it possible to distribute a time signal from the Royal Observatory at Greenwich to stations across the country. By 1880, Greenwich Mean Time was the legal time throughout Great Britain. In North America, where the distances were much larger, the railways introduced standard time zones in 1883, and an international conference in Washington the following year chose the meridian through Greenwich as the prime meridian for the world.

The twentieth century replaced the swinging pendulum with vibrating crystals and then with atoms. A piece of quartz, cut in the right way, bends slightly when a voltage is applied to it and produces a voltage when it is bent. Placed in a suitable circuit, a quartz crystal will vibrate at a very stable frequency. The first quartz clock was built at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1927, and by the 1930s quartz clocks in observatories were more stable than the best pendulums. They were stable enough, in fact, to reveal that the rotation of the earth itself is not perfectly regular. The day lengthens and shortens by small amounts over the course of a year, and it is slowly growing longer over the centuries as the tides drain energy from the earth's spin.

If the earth was no longer a good enough clock, something better was needed to define the second. The answer came from atomic physics. Atoms of a given element absorb and emit radiation at frequencies that are, as far as anyone can tell, identical for every atom of that kind, everywhere and always. In 1955 Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the National Physical Laboratory in England built the first practical caesium clock, which locked a microwave oscillator to a transition in caesium atoms. In 1967 the second was redefined as the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation corresponding to that transition. A unit that had been a fraction of the day became a property of an atom, and the day became something measured in seconds rather than the other way round.

Atomic time and the rotating earth now drift apart by a little under a second over a year or two. To keep civil time close to the sun, an extra second, the leap second, has occasionally been inserted at the end of June or December. Leap seconds are a nuisance for computer systems, which tend to assume that every minute has sixty seconds, and several large outages have been traced to software that mishandled one. In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035, accepting a slowly growing gap between atomic time and the sun in exchange for a time scale without discontinuities.

Today's best clocks no longer use microwaves at all. Optical clocks trap single ions or clouds of neutral atoms and probe them with lasers at frequencies hundreds of thousands of times higher than the caesium transition. Their ticks are correspondingly finer, and the best of them would neither gain nor lose a second over a span longer than the age of the universe. At that level of precision, a clock becomes a sensitive instrument in its own right. Because gravity slows the passage of time, two optical clocks whose heights differ by a few centimetres can be seen to run at measurably different rates, exactly as general relativity predicts. Geodesists have begun to talk about using networks of such clocks to map the shape of the earth's gravitational field.

Most people never see any of this machinery, but they rely on it constantly. The satellites of the global navigation systems each carry atomic clocks, and a receiver on the ground works out its position by comparing the times at which signals left several satellites. An error of a millionth of a second would put the receiver about three hundred metres from where it actually is. Mobile phone networks, power grids, and financial exchanges all depend on time signals distributed from atomic clocks, and a modern data centre may synchronise its servers to within a few microseconds so that the order of events recorded in different machines can be trusted.

It is easy to forget how recent all of this is. For thousands of years, an hour was a loose and seasonal thing, and a minute was hardly a unit at all. The equal hour arrived with the tower clock, the minute with the pendulum, the shared national time with the railway and the telegraph, and the second as we now define it with the atom. Each step was driven by a practical need, whether it was the ringing of a monastery bell, the safety of a ship, or the timetable of a train, and each step changed the way people organised their lives. The stick in the ground still works, of course. On a clear day it will tell you, to within a few minutes, roughly when noon has come, which for most of history was all that anyone needed to know.

The story of timekeeping is also a story about trust. A clock is useful only if the people who read it agree that it is right, and much of the history above is about how that agreement was reached. A medieval town trusted its bell because the council paid a keeper to wind the clock and correct it against a sundial. A ship's captain trusted his chronometer because it had been rated, that is, its daily gain or loss had been measured over weeks at an observatory before the voyage, so that its known error could be subtracted each day at sea. A railway clerk trusted the telegraph signal from Greenwich because the observatory checked its own clocks every clear night against the transit of stars across the meridian. Each method moved the burden of proof somewhere else, to a better clock, a more careful observation, or a more stable physical process, until today the final reference is the behaviour of atoms, compared and averaged across laboratories on several continents.

That averaging is itself a substantial piece of work. No single clock defines international atomic time. Instead, several hundred clocks in dozens of national laboratories report their readings to the International Bureau of Weights and Measures near Paris, which combines them into a weighted average. Clocks that have been more stable in the recent past receive more weight, and the most accurate primary standards are used to steer the average so that its second stays close to the definition. The result is published monthly, after the fact, as a list of corrections that each laboratory can apply to its own realisation of time. In other words, the official time of the world is known precisely only in retrospect, a few weeks late, while the time that everyone actually uses in the moment is a prediction of what that average will turn out to have been.
